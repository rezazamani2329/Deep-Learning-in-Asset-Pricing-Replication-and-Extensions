SLIDE 3: The paper

Explain: The SDF prices assets through the restriction that its product with excess returns has zero conditional expectation. Firm characteristics describe stocks, while macroeconomic history describes the economy. The paper combines these inputs with economic pricing restrictions. Finding: the slide reports monthly test Sharpe of 0.75 for GAN versus 0.19 for FF3. Monthly units matter throughout this comparison. Source: original-paper results summarized in our replication materials.

The SDF is an economic weighting of future payoffs, with greater relevance for payoffs delivered when they are especially valuable. For excess returns, the pricing condition is E[M times R excess | information] = 0. The model learns weights through these pricing moments rather than directly maximizing realized test Sharpe. The portfolio Sharpe is a useful performance summary, but it does not by itself establish that every asset is correctly priced.
Question: Why include macro data? Answer: Identical firm characteristics can have different implications in different economic states. The macro encoder provides state information to the firm-level network.
Transition: The next slide shows how the paper connects these economic restrictions to trainable neural networks.

SLIDE 4: Model Architecture

Explain the learning path: The SDF macro LSTM compresses 178 macro series into four states. The feedforward network combines these states with 46 characteristics to generate stock weights. Weighted subsequent excess returns determine a factor and M = 1 minus that factor. Pricing errors propagate gradients through both networks. A separate macro encoder produces 32 states for eight conditional instruments. Training first fits unconditional pricing, then freezes the pricing model while the adversary finds difficult moments, and finally freezes the adversary while the pricing model reduces those errors. End-to-end learning connects economic loss to learned representations and portfolio weights.

Presenter walkthrough: Begin with information available at month t. Feed the history of macro variables to the SDF encoder. Combine its four learned states with each stock's 46 characteristics. The two 64-unit feedforward layers output a weight for each stock. Subsequent excess returns enter the training pricing moments, with the learned factor F(t+1) equal to the sum of weight times excess return. The SDF is M(t+1) = 1 - F(t+1).
The adversary uses a separate encoder with 32 macro states and combines these with firm characteristics to produce eight instruments. It tries to identify conditional moments that the pricing network handles poorly. The pricing network then tries to reduce those errors. Freeze one side while training the other.
Backpropagation connects the loss to the weight network and macro encoder, so the state representation learns to serve the economic objective. The adversary has its own learning path. This is the end-to-end learning process.
Question: Does the model see next month's return when generating a weight? Answer: Future realized returns enter the training loss, while weight inputs must contain only information available at the decision date.

Transition: The first extension changes how the model reads macroeconomic history. Introduce attention as an alternative to recurrent memory.



SLIDE 8: Extension 1: Transformer instead of LSTM

Explain: The LSTM updates a recurrent state month by month. The Transformer projects the macro inputs into representations, adds positional information and uses attention over available history. A causal mask prevents access to future months. We replace both macro encoders with two-layer, four-head Transformers while keeping output dimensions of four and 32. We retrain the LSTM with the same nine seeds and reduced schedule. Attention may capture distant relationships, but the performance benefit is an empirical question. Source: Code 08 and src/models/transformer_gan_paper.py.

Attention compares learned representations of different months and assigns weights to their relevance. Positional information distinguishes time order. The causal mask permits a month to attend only to information available up to that month. Both the SDF and conditional macro encoders change, while the economic output dimensions remain four and 32.
A seed controls random initialization and random draws. Nine seeds produce nine members per architecture, not nine different economic model classes. Their ensemble reduces dependence on a single initialization.
Question: Why did Transformer not improve Sharpe? Answer: We observe lower mean return and higher volatility under this budget. Possible explanations include limited macro training history, architecture tuning and insufficient training, but this experiment does not identify the cause. Equal budgets control effort rather than guarantee equal convergence.

SLIDE 15: Extension 3: economic regimes

Explain: We group the saved test returns into 274 expansion and 26 recession months using NBER labels. Volatility groups contain 123 high and 177 low months, based on lagged twelve-month market volatility and a threshold set using training data. Lagging the volatility measure avoids future information. NBER classifications serve as retrospective evaluation labels. These groups do not involve separate regime-trained models. Source: Codes 10 and 012.

Detailed process: Start with the same 300 monthly test returns for every portfolio. Attach a business-cycle label and a volatility label to each month. For each group, compute the mean return and its standard deviation using only the months in that group, then calculate monthly conditional Sharpe. Expansion and recession partition the test sample, as do high and low volatility. They are separate classifications, and they need not select identical months.
Business-cycle definition: NBER labels identify 274 expansion months and 26 recession months. These are 91.3 percent and 8.7 percent of the sample. Recession observations can come from distinct episodes separated by years. They are not a single contiguous 26-month investment period. The labels describe the economy retrospectively, and this analysis does not claim that all final NBER labels were observable in real time.
Volatility definition: Use a market-volatility estimate based on the past twelve months. Compare it with a threshold determined from training data. The saved test classification gives 123 high-volatility months, or 41 percent, and 177 low-volatility months, or 59 percent. Lagging the estimate and fixing the threshold before the test period avoids using future returns to set the volatility label.
Why this matters: Aggregate performance can hide weak results during stress. A strategy with a high full-test Sharpe may still deliver poorly precisely when investors need it. The two groupings ask different questions: business-cycle weakness and financial-market turbulence are related but not identical.
Interpretation limits: Twenty-six recession months provide much less information than 274 expansion months. Estimates can depend strongly on a few returns. Recession or volatility labels do not randomize exposure, so this comparison does not identify the causal effect of recessions on a model. Sharpe also combines changes in average return and dispersion, and should be read with those components where available.
Question: Is this a recession-specific model? Answer: No. The same saved portfolio is evaluated in different groups. Question: Can I compare these Sharpe values to the rolling column? Answer: These are monthly group Sharpe ratios. Rolling values elsewhere are annualized unless explicitly converted.
Transition: Having defined the groups, compare the model values within each group rather than across inconsistent units.

SLIDE 17: Extension 4: Uncertainty

Transition: The rankings so far are point estimates. We now measure their sampling uncertainty.

Suggested introduction: A ranking is not enough. We have observed returns from one historical period, and the estimated Sharpe could change in another sample. Extension 4 quantifies this sampling uncertainty for fixed saved portfolios.

SLIDE 18: Extension 4: how sure are we? Bootstrap uncertainty

Explain: Resample paired blocks of monthly test returns so every model receives the same sampled months and dependence within blocks is retained. Full-test comparisons use 10,000 replicates with twelve-month blocks. Rolling-window uncertainty uses 2,000 replicates and six-month blocks. Confidence intervals assess sampling variability of fixed saved strategies. They do not capture all uncertainty from retraining, seed selection or model search. Compare the interval for the paired gap directly, since overlapping individual intervals alone do not resolve significance. Source: Code 012.

Detailed bootstrap procedure: First compute each portfolio's monthly Sharpe on the actual test data. Select contiguous blocks of months from that history, wrapping at the sample boundary in a circular block bootstrap. Combine sampled blocks to obtain a resampled history of the required length. Use exactly the same month indices for every model. Recompute each Sharpe and each paired difference. Repeat many times to obtain distributions of these estimates.
Why blocks: Financial returns can have time dependence. Sampling independent individual months breaks dependence within nearby observations. Blocks retain dependence within the selected segment, although no block choice can perfectly reproduce every feature of the original series.
Why paired sampling: When both models perform well in the same month, that co-movement matters for the variability of their difference. Pairing retains it. Independently bootstrapping each model would lose this relationship and answer a less useful question.
Full-test configuration: Code 012 uses 10,000 replicates and twelve-month blocks as the primary full-test specification, with six- and twenty-four-month choices for sensitivity. Sensitivity describes dependence on the procedure, and should not be used to choose whichever method gives a favorable result.
Rolling configuration: Each complete 36- or 60-month window receives its own bootstrap calculation using 2,000 replicates and six-month blocks. The rolling exercise therefore differs from the full-test settings. The main slide simplifies the process, so state this distinction if asked.
Confidence interpretation: A frequentist 95 percent confidence procedure aims for nominal coverage over repeated samples under its assumptions. Do not describe one realized interval as a 95 percent probability that a fixed true parameter lies inside it. In practice, use it to describe the range of values consistent with the uncertainty procedure.
Basic interval: If theta-hat is the observed statistic and q-low and q-high are bootstrap quantiles, the basic interval reflects the bootstrap quantiles around the observed estimate: [2 times theta-hat minus q-high, 2 times theta-hat minus q-low]. This differs from a percentile interval that directly uses bootstrap quantiles.
What this does not measure: We bootstrap saved returns, not new training runs. The intervals do not fully represent initialization uncertainty, hyperparameter search, test-informed model selection or future structural changes. Blocks also do not make a small recession sample large.
Question: Do overlapping individual intervals mean there is no significant difference? Answer: Not necessarily. Directly bootstrap the paired Sharpe difference. Correlation between model returns affects its uncertainty.
Transition: Read model-level rolling bands first, then use the paired-difference table for the ranking question.

Calculation: Blend return in each month is 0.5 times LSTM return plus 0.5 times Transformer return. Blend Sharpe is calculated from the resulting series, not from averaging the two individual Sharpes. SLIDE 26: Summary: findings and next steps

Closing: We contribute a common-budget Transformer comparison and evaluate saved portfolios across rolling windows, economic regimes and bootstrap uncertainty. LSTM has higher observed test Sharpe than Transformer, but paired intervals leave the advantage unresolved. Combining architectures reduces observed volatility with little Sharpe improvement. Next steps include the full author training schedule on GPU, a fresh holdout, transaction-cost evaluation and uncertainty across training runs. Invite questions about the distinction between economic learning and performance evaluation.

Closing statement: We retain the economic pricing framework while changing the macro encoder and broadening performance evaluation. The evidence supports a higher observed LSTM Sharpe under the completed matched budget. It does not establish a universal architecture ranking. Combining models reduces observed risk, with little reliable Sharpe gain.
Research priorities: Complete the full audited schedule for both architectures on suitable hardware. Evaluate choices on a new holdout. Estimate turnover and transaction costs using actual portfolio weights. Examine variation across independent training runs, since fixed-return bootstraps do not capture all learning uncertainty.
Question: Why not conclude that Transformer is worse? Answer: The observed result favors LSTM, but it comes from one sample, reduced training and statistically unresolved gaps. That is a useful finding with a defined scope.
